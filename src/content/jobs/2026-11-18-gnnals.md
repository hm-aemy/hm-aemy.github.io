## 1. Approximate Computing

In modern computing systems, simultaneously optimizing power consumption, silicon area, and delay has become increasingly challenging, particularly as traditional semiconductor scaling approaches its physical limits. Meanwhile, the growing demand for energy-efficient embedded and IoT devices calls for alternative design approaches. Fortunately, many applications, including digital signal processing, multimedia, and data mining, can tolerate a certain degree of computational inaccuracy. This has motivated **Approximate Computing (AC)**, a paradigm that trades computational accuracy for improvements in power, area, and delay [1]. However, introducing approximation error as an additional design constraint significantly increases the complexity of circuit optimization and expands the design space.

The process of automatic generation of approximate circuits, using an exact netlist as a starting point, is known as Approximate Logic Synthesis (or simply, ALS). A typical ALS workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1 [2]:

![Typical ALS workflow](/assets/img/gnnals/typical-als-workflow.png)

*Figure 1. A typical ALS tool workflow [2]*

An ALS tool typically takes an exact circuit as input and follows three key steps: (1) **Error Modeling** estimates the impact of each circuit section (e.g., gates) on the final output, providing valuable guidance for design space exploration. While *optional*, it significantly enhances the tool’s performance. **(2) ALS Method** is the core algorithm that generates resource-efficient approximate designs by exploring the design space, optimizing for power, area, and delay compared to the exact circuit. **(3) QoR Evaluation** ensures that the introduced error remains within acceptable limits by verifying the quality of results against required constraints.

## 2. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) Are Apparently Useful!

Meanwhile, recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), offer new opportunities for addressing these challenges. On the one hand, digital circuits can naturally be represented as Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs), where nodes represent logic gates and edges represent their interconnections. On the other hand, GNNs can capture structural and functional relationships within graphs and learn to predict the impact of Local Approximate Changes (LACs) on circuit error and hardware efficiency. This learned information could guide the selection of promising approximate transformations, enabling a more efficient exploration of the vast design space.

## 3. A Bit of Background

Broadly speaking, when exploring the potential of AC at the gate level, the first step is to identify subparts of a circuit (subcircuits) that are theoretically suitable candidates for approximation; which are called Local Approximate Changes (LACs). A LAC is deemed suitable if applying it would ideally introduce low amounts of error while saving a substantial amount of hardware resources. One may categorize them broadly in two (non-mutually exclusive) groups of techniques: (1) Gate-level Pruning: where the candidate subcircuits are simply pruned out of the netlist [3], and (2) Circuit Transformations: in which the selected subcircuits are not simply carved out but are actually replaced with with moderately or substantially simplified versions [4].  To the best of my knowledge, many established methods use *heuristic* or *manually designed* algorithms to achieve their goals, and no single best solution exists. with no universally optimal strategy across different circuits and error constraints. Consequently, identifying promising approximation candidates remains a challenging task, motivating the exploration of alternative, data-driven approaches to guide this process.

## 4. The Way We Envision This Project

Our initial vision for GNNALS begins with generating a comprehensive database of approximate circuits using existing state-of-the-art ALS methods, covering both gate-level pruning and circuit transformation techniques. This database would serve as the foundation for training a GNN to learn which LACs are most promising in terms of approximation error and hardware area (or possibly the power consumption or delay) savings. Once trained, the GNN would be integrated into an iterative synthesis framework that takes an exact circuit and a predefined error budget as inputs, identifies applicable LACs, and uses the learned model to guide their selection and application. Through successive approximation and evaluation steps, the framework would aim to produce circuits with favorable error–area trade-offs. **This workflow represents our initial vision rather than a fixed methodology**, and students are encouraged to explore alternative approaches, propose improvements, or even rethink the overall strategy to achieve more effective and efficient results. ## 5. What Might the Outcomes Look Like?

As with any exploratory research project, there is no guarantee that our proposed approach will outperform existing methods. We therefore envision two possible scenarios:

**Best-Case Scenario:** Our GNN-guided approach proves effective, producing approximate circuits with superior error–area and error–power trade-offs compared to state-of-the-art methods. Additionally, we quantitatively demonstrate the differences between gate-level pruning and circuit transformation techniques, providing valuable insights into their respective strengths and limitations.

![Best-case error–power/area trade-off](/assets/img/gnnals/best-case-scenario.png)

**Worst-Case Scenario:** Our approach does not achieve competitive results compared to existing methods. Nevertheless, this would still constitute a meaningful research outcome. Through systematic experiments and analysis, we would investigate why the approach did not perform as expected, identify its limitations, and document our findings to guide future research.
